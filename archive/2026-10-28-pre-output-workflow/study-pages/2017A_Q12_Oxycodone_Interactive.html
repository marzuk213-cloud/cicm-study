① Pharmaceutics

  • Class: Semi-synthetic opioid (thebaine derivative)
  • Receptors: Predominant μ (MOP) agonist — some κ (KOP), δ (DOP) activity. All Gi-protein coupled.
  • Chemistry: Weak base; pKₐ ~8.5–8.9; logP ~0.7 (low-moderate lipophilicity)
  • Presentations:
    • IR oral (Endone)
    • CR/PR (OxyContin) — biphasic, 12-hr duration
    • Oxycodone/naloxone PR (Targin) — oral naloxone BA <3% → gut antagonism only
    • IV/SC for ICU
  • Potency: ~1.5–2× oral morphine equipotent
Endogenous ligands (worth a buzzword): β-endorphin/endomorphins → μ; enkephalins → δ; dynorphins → κ. The MOP receptor mediates analgesia; other subtypes mediate side effects.

② Pharmacodynamics — MoA Pathway

Click "Next step" to walk the signalling cascade. Examiners reward both pre- and postsynaptic detail.

1
μ (MOP) receptor agonism
Gi-protein coupled receptor on neurons in dorsal horn, PAG, thalamus, limbic system.
2
Gi activation → ↓ adenylate cyclase → ↓ cAMP
Reduces neuronal excitability via downstream second messenger reduction.
3
Presynaptic: closes voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels
↓ Ca²⁺ influx → ↓ vesicular release of glutamate and Substance P at primary afferent terminals.
4
Postsynaptic: opens K⁺ channels
K⁺ efflux → membrane hyperpolarisation → reduced action potential generation in second-order neurons.
5
Net effect: ↓ nociceptive transmission (spinal + supraspinal)
Plus altered affective perception of pain via limbic/cortical effects. Descending modulation from PAG → RVM → dorsal horn is augmented.

③ Pharmacokinetics — A·D·E

Absorption

  • Oral BA: 60-87% (vs morphine 20-30%) — limited first-pass
  • IR Tmax: ~1–1.5 h
  • CR/PR: biphasic absorption, ~12-hr duration
  • Targin: oral naloxone BA <3% → gut μ-antagonism without systemic reversal

Distribution

  • Vd: ~2.6 L/kg
  • Protein binding: ~38–45% (albumin + α1-acid glycoprotein)
  • Crosses placenta · present in breast milk
  • Pregnancy ↑ CYP2D6 activity ~50% → ↑ oxymorphone formation

Excretion

  • Primarily renal as parent + metabolites
  • ~20% unchanged · ~50% conjugated · ~14% via oxymorphone pathway
  • t½: IR ~3–5 h · PR steady state ~24–36 h
  • Active metabolites accumulate in renal failure → dose reduce

Key interaction: A CYP3A4 inhibitor (e.g. clarithromycin, ketoconazole, ritonavir, grapefruit) → significant ↑ clinical effect. Why? 3A4 is the major pathway; inhibition diverts more drug through CYP2D6 → ↑ oxymorphone AND ↑ parent levels.
CYP2D6 polymorphism (deberisoquine): Poor metabolisers → ↓ oxymorphone → ↓ analgesia. Ultra-rapid metabolisers → ↑ oxymorphone → toxicity risk. Same enzyme that activates codeine and tramadol.

⑤ Toxicity & Reversal

Opioid toxidrome: Coma + miosis + respiratory depression (the classic triad).
  • Naloxone: pure μ-antagonist, IV 40-400 mcg titrated
  • t½ naloxone ~30-90 min — much shorter than oxycodone CR → repeat dosing or infusion often needed
  • Acute reversal risks: precipitated withdrawal, sympathetic surge, pulmonary oedema

🃏 Active Recall — Flashcards

Q: Why does oxycodone have higher oral BA than morphine?
A: Limited first-pass metabolism. Oxycodone 60-87% vs morphine 20-30%. Makes it predictable for enteral step-down.
Q: Two CYP enzymes that metabolise oxycodone — products and activity?
A: CYP3A4 → noroxycodone (inactive, ~45% of dose). CYP2D6 → oxymorphone (active, 14× μ-potency, ~10% of dose).
Q: What does a CYP3A4 inhibitor do to oxycodone clinical effect?
A: Significant increase. Diverts drug through CYP2D6 → ↑ oxymorphone (active) AND ↑ parent drug levels.
Q: Why does oxycodone accumulate in renal failure?
A: Active metabolites (oxymorphone) are renally excreted and accumulate. Same problem as morphine (M6G), pethidine, tramadol. Dose reduce.
Q: Describe the CO₂ response curve effect of opioids vs benzos.
A: Opioids — shifted right (reduced ventilatory drive at any PaCO₂). Benzos — flattened (no shift, just reduced slope).
Q: μ-receptor signalling cascade?
A: Gi → ↓ adenylate cyclase → ↓ cAMP. Presynaptic: closes Ca²⁺ channels → ↓ glutamate/Substance P release. Postsynaptic: opens K⁺ channels → hyperpolarisation.
Q: How does Targin (oxycodone/naloxone PR) work?
A: Oral naloxone has <3% BA due to extensive first-pass — provides gut μ-antagonism (reduces constipation) without reversing systemic analgesia.
Q: Three sources of "first-pass" reducing oxycodone bioavailability?
A: Gut bacteria · gut wall enzymes · hepatic extraction. Combined first-pass, not just hepatic.
Q: Effect of ageing on oxycodone PK?
A: ↓ first-pass metabolism → ↑ oral BA → start low, titrate slowly.
Q: Naloxone t½ vs oxycodone t½ — clinical implication?
A: Naloxone t½ 30-90 min; oxycodone CR much longer. Repeat dosing or infusion needed to avoid re-narcotisation.
Q: Which opioid is sequestered ~75% by pulmonary first-pass?
A: Fentanyl (not oxycodone). Affects onset/duration kinetics.
Q: Endogenous opioid ligands for μ, δ, κ?
A: μ → β-endorphin, endomorphins. δ → enkephalins (also some μ). κ → dynorphins. All Gi-coupled.
