⚡ Quick-Glance SAQ Framework (write the headings first)

The question asks for the pharmacological basis. Give every treatment a mechanism sentence that starts with “because”. Don't just write a resus list. The weights below are a suggested time split, not official marks.

📝 10-minute model answer skeleton (tap to open)
Management stepPharmacological basis
Mechanism of toxicityOPs are substrate analogues. The active-site serine of AChE is phosphorylated. The phosphoryl-enzyme hydrolyses over hours to days, against microseconds for the acetyl-enzyme, so ACh accumulates. Result: excess muscarinic, nicotinic (NMJ + ganglia) and CNS stimulation. Many pesticides are thions that need CYP oxidative desulfuration to the active oxon.
Resuscitation & airwayMost deaths are respiratory: central depression + bronchorrhoea/bronchospasm + NMJ failure (± aspiration). Intubate early if needed. Avoid suxamethonium: it is metabolised by plasma BuChE, which is inhibited, so paralysis is prolonged. Use a non-depolarising agent.
DecontaminationReduces further absorption. Remove clothes and wash skin. Lavage or charcoal have no proven benefit and come only after stabilisation.
AtropineCompetitive (surmountable) muscarinic antagonist. The ACh load is huge and unknown, so titrate: 1.8–3 mg IV, double every ~5 min until the chest is clear, HR > 80, SBP > 80, axillae are dry and pupils are no longer pinpoint. Then infuse 10–20% of the loading dose per hour. Tertiary amine, so some central effect. No nicotinic effect.
Oxime (pralidoxime)Reactivates AChE. The quaternary pyridinium docks at the anionic site and the oxime makes a nucleophilic attack on the phosphorus, giving phosphoryl-oxime + free enzyme. It restores nicotinic as well as muscarinic function. It only works before ageing (dealkylation; t½ ≈ 3.7 h dimethyl vs 33 h diethyl) and while OP isn't re-inhibiting the enzyme. Poor CNS entry. Evidence is mixed (WHO regimen: ≥ 30 mg/kg load, then 8–10 mg/kg/h).
BenzodiazepinesGABAA positive allosteric modulation, used for seizures and agitation. Animal data suggest neuroprotection and less central respiratory failure.
Adjuncts (investigational)Mg²⁺ blocks presynaptic Ca²⁺ entry, so less ACh is released. Clonidine (α₂) also reduces ACh release. NaHCO₃, FFP (BuChE scavenger, stoichiometrically overwhelmed) and haemofiltration lack good evidence.
Monitoring & complicationsClinical endpoints first. RBC AChE reflects synaptic activity and severity; plasma BuChE is an exposure marker only. Watch for intermediate syndrome (24–96 h), recurrence with lipid-soluble OPs and OPIDN.

The chemistry in four lines

① Normal hydrolysis (µs)
E–OH + ACh → E–O–acetyl + choline
E–O–acetyl + H₂O → E–OH + acetate
Acetyl-enzyme regenerates in microseconds.
② Phosphorylation
E–OH + (RO)₂P(=O)–X → E–O–P(=O)(OR)₂ + HX
The leaving group X departs. The phosphoryl-enzyme hydrolyses only over hours to days.
③ Oxime reactivation
E–O–P(OR)₂ + Ox–OH → E–OH + Ox–O–P(OR)₂
The oxime oxygen attacks the phosphorus. Enzyme is freed and phosphoryl-oxime leaves.
④ Ageing (dealkylation)
E–O–P(=O)(OR)₂ → E–O–P(=O)(OR)(O⁻) + R⁺
Loss of one alkyl group leaves an anionic, stabilised adduct that oximes can't reactivate.

Carbamates (e.g. carbaryl) carbamylate the same serine. That enzyme hydrolyses spontaneously over minutes to hours and doesn't age, so oximes aren't recommended for carbamate poisoning.2

Mnemonics: muscarinic = DUMBELS (Diarrhoea, Urination, Miosis, Bronchorrhoea/Bronchospasm/Bradycardia, Emesis, Lacrimation, Salivation/Sweating). The killer Bs (bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm, bradycardia) are what you titrate atropine against. Nicotinic: fasciculations → weakness, with tachycardia and hypertension from the ganglia.
Why HR and pupils are unreliable: they sit at the balance between muscarinic (slow, small) and ganglionic/sympathetic (fast, big) drive. Hypoxia, hypovolaemia, alcohol withdrawal and oximes also push HR up. Tachycardia is not a reason to withhold atropine when the chest is wet.2

What “ageing” is

  • The phosphoryl-enzyme loses one alkyl/alkoxy group (dealkylation).
  • This leaves a negatively charged adduct, stabilised in the active site, that resists nucleophilic attack by the oxime.
  • Recovery then needs new enzyme synthesis, which takes days to weeks.

What the simple curve leaves out

  • Re-inhibition: while OP is still in the blood (large ingestions, lipid-soluble OPs) it re-phosphorylates any enzyme that has been reactivated.1
  • Spontaneous reactivation: diethyl compounds both reactivate and age more slowly than dimethyl.4
  • S-alkyl OPs (profenofos) aren't reactivated at all. Nerve agents like soman age within minutes.1,6

Pharmacological reason for doubling: atropine competes with a huge and unknown ACh concentration (competitive antagonism shifts the agonist curve to the right), so no fixed dose fits. Doubling can deliver about 70 mg in under 30 min. Repeating small boluses can take hours, and severely poisoned patients die in that time.1,3
Atropine toxicity (agitation or confusion, hyperthermia, absent bowel sounds, urinary retention): stop the infusion, review after 30 min, and restart at 70–80% of the previous rate once settled.2

🧰 Other Management Pharmacology

🧠 Benzodiazepines (diazepam)

  • GABAA positive allosteric modulator → ↑ Cl⁻ channel opening frequency → hyperpolarisation.
  • First line for seizures (more common with nerve agents and in hypoxic patients) and for agitated delirium, which can come from the OP, atropine toxicity, hypoxia or alcohol.
  • Animal data: ↓ neural damage, ↓ central respiratory failure.1
  • Preferred over haloperidol, which is pro-convulsant, prolongs QT, impairs thermoregulation and needs large doses alongside atropine.2

🫁 Airway & neuromuscular blockers

  • Respiratory failure is the usual cause of death (central + muscarinic + NMJ), so intubate and ventilate as needed.
  • Avoid suxamethonium: it is hydrolysed by plasma BuChE, which is inhibited, so the block is prolonged (case reports of prolonged paralysis).9
  • Use a non-depolarising agent. It competes with excess ACh at the NMJ, so the response may be less predictable: monitor.

🧼 Decontamination

  • Skin: remove clothing and wash. Staff use universal precautions and good ventilation; secondary poisoning hasn't been shown biochemically.1
  • GI: no proven benefit. Only after resuscitation. Consider lavage only if the patient presents early (within 1–2 h) and is intubated or cooperative.
  • Activated charcoal: an RCT of > 1000 pesticide patients found no benefit. Ipecac is contraindicated (rapid coma → aspiration).1,2

🧲 Magnesium sulfate investigational

  • Blocks presynaptic Ca²⁺ channels → ↓ ACh release → better NMJ function. Also ↓ NMDA-mediated CNS overstimulation.
  • Evidence: one small, non-randomised study showed lower mortality. Treat with caution.1

🔬 Other adjuncts investigational

  • Clonidine (α₂ agonist): ↓ ACh synthesis and release. Animal data only.
  • NaHCO₃ alkalinisation: Cochrane found insufficient evidence.
  • FFP as a BuChE scavenger: binds OP 1:1, so it is overwhelmed. 20 g of dimethoate would need about 6 kg of BuChE.
  • Haemofiltration: role unclear. Recombinant phosphotriesterases are a future option.1

🩸 Cholinesterase assays (monitoring)

  • RBC AChE is the same enzyme as at synapses, so it is a good marker of synaptic function and severity. Activity ≥ 30% of normal → normal muscle function, no atropine needed. < 10% → grossly deranged NMJ function and high atropine needs.1
  • Plasma BuChE is a sensitive marker of exposure but doesn't track severity.
  • Samples must be cooled and diluted immediately, otherwise inhibition or reactivation continues in the tube. Results rarely arrive in time to guide care: treat the clinical picture.

⚠️ Common Ways to Drop Marks

1. A management list with no pharmacology. “Atropine, pralidoxime, diazepam” earns little. Each needs a receptor or enzyme + mechanism + consequence.
2. “Atropine reverses the paralysis.” It doesn't. It is muscarinic only. Weakness is nicotinic: oxime (if not aged) plus ventilation.
3. Wrong atropine endpoints. Don't chase tachycardia or dilated pupils. Titrate to drying of secretions with HR > 80 and SBP > 80.
4. Oximes without ageing. State the time-dependence, the dimethyl vs diethyl difference, re-inhibition and the uncertain evidence.
5. Forgetting suxamethonium. BuChE inhibition prolongs the block. This is an easy, high-yield sentence.
6. Ignoring the CNS and later syndromes. Oximes don't enter the brain, so benzos and the airway cover it. Watch for intermediate syndrome after the crisis has settled.

🃏 Flashcards: tap to flip

Why does an OP effectively inhibit AChE irreversibly, when ACh (the normal substrate) doesn't?
Both form a covalent bond with the active-site serine. The acetyl-enzyme hydrolyses in microseconds. The phosphoryl-enzyme hydrolyses over hours to days, and ageing then makes it permanent.
What does atropine NOT treat?
Nicotinic effects: fasciculations, weakness, respiratory muscle paralysis (NMJ) and ganglionic tachycardia or hypertension. Its CNS effect is only partial.
Five atropinisation endpoints?
Clear chest on auscultation · HR > 80 · SBP > 80 mmHg · dry axillae · pupils no longer pinpoint. The chest, HR and BP matter most.
Pharmacological reason for doubling atropine boluses?
It is a competitive antagonist against a huge, unknown ACh load, and requirements range from 1 to over 70 mg. Doubling reaches about 70 mg in under 30 min; repeating small doses takes hours.
How does pralidoxime reactivate AChE?
The quaternary pyridinium N⁺ binds the anionic site, positioning the oxime. The oxime O makes a nucleophilic attack on the phosphorus, phosphoryl-oxime leaves, and the serine-OH is free again.
Define ageing and give the half-lives.
Dealkylation of the phosphoryl-enzyme gives an anionic, stabilised, oxime-resistant adduct. Human RBC AChE t½: 3.7 h dimethyl, 33 h diethyl.
Why do oximes do little for CNS toxicity?
They are quaternary and permanently charged, so they penetrate the BBB poorly. Central features need atropine (tertiary amine), benzodiazepines and airway support.
Why avoid suxamethonium for RSI?
It is metabolised by plasma BuChE, which the OP has inhibited, so paralysis is prolonged. Use a non-depolarising agent and monitor.
Signs of atropine toxicity and what to do?
Agitation or confusion, hyperthermia, absent bowel sounds, urinary retention, tachycardia. Stop the infusion, review at 30 min, restart at 70–80% of the previous rate.
RBC AChE vs plasma BuChE: which tracks severity?
RBC AChE (same enzyme as at synapses): ≥ 30% means normal NMJ function, < 10% means severe. BuChE is a sensitive marker of exposure but doesn't track severity.
Intermediate syndrome: timing, features, mechanism?
24–96 h, after the cholinergic crisis. Neck flexor weakness, cranial nerve palsies, proximal limb and respiratory muscle weakness. NMJ dysfunction from persistent AChE inhibition and receptor changes. Atropine doesn't help: ventilate.
Carbamates vs OPs: pharmacological difference?
Carbamates carbamylate the serine. The enzyme hydrolyses spontaneously over minutes to hours and doesn't age, so the illness is shorter and oximes aren't recommended.
Why does onset of toxicity get delayed with some OPs (e.g. parathion)?
They are thions (P=S), which need hepatic CYP oxidative desulfuration to the active oxon (P=O). Lipid-soluble OPs (e.g. fenthion) also redistribute from fat and cause recurrence.
Proposed mechanism of magnesium in OP poisoning?
It blocks presynaptic Ca²⁺ channels, so less ACh is released (better NMJ function), and it reduces NMDA-mediated CNS excitation. Evidence: one small non-randomised study.
